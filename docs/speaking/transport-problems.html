Speaking test

Answer Guide: Transport Problems in Your Hometown

Choose Topic A if your hometown has lots of transport problems, or Topic B if it has few. Each question below comes with idea banks, a model answer of about 150 words, and useful language, so you can speak for a full 1.5 to 2 minutes.

1. The answer formula

A strong answer has five parts, and each one adds 1 or 2 sentences. Together they fill about 150 words.

  1. Direct answer. Answer the question in your first sentence, using the words of the question.
  2. Main reason. Give your biggest reason and explain it (because, which means, so).
  3. Second reason. Add another angle: people, money, geography, history or habits.
  4. Specific example. Describe a real street, a real moment or a daily routine. Details make you sound natural.
  5. Result or closing thought. Say what the effect is, or what you hope will happen.

Ideas that impress examiners come from looking at a problem from several sides: people (habits, age groups), infrastructure (roads, buses), economy (cost, jobs), environment (pollution, noise) and time (rush hour, holidays, the next 10 years).

2. Topic A: My hometown has lots of transport problems

Question 1: Why does your hometown have lots of transport problems?

Idea bank

  • People: Most families own at least one motorbike or car, because buses feel slow and unreliable. Many parents drive their children to school, so the roads are packed at the same hour every day.
  • Infrastructure: The streets were designed decades ago for far fewer vehicles. Roadworks last for months, and there are not enough bridges or parking spaces.
  • Growth: The city has grown quickly because of tourism and new factories, and the population has doubled while the roads have barely changed.
  • Habits: Drivers park on pavements, ignore red lights or cut through traffic, which creates bottlenecks and accidents.
  • Effects: Long commutes, stress, noise, air pollution and lost working hours.

Model answer (about 190 words)

My hometown has a lot of transport problems, and I think there are three main reasons. First of all, the city has grown extremely fast in the last ten years. More people have moved here for work, and tourism has boomed, but the roads are still the same ones that were built for a much smaller population. As a result, the main streets are completely overloaded.

Another reason is that public transport is not very attractive. The buses are infrequent and often late, so almost everyone prefers to buy a motorbike or a car. That means that during rush hour, thousands of private vehicles try to squeeze through the same narrow roads at the same time.

To give you an example, the bridge near my house is the only way to get to the city centre, and every morning it takes me about forty minutes to travel just three kilometres. On top of that, some drivers park on the pavement or ignore the traffic lights, which makes the situation even worse.

The result is that people waste a lot of time, the air is polluted, and everyone feels stressed. That's why I would say transport is one of the biggest challenges in my hometown right now.

Question 2: What advice do you have for your hometown about the transport problems?

Idea bank

  • Public transport: Run clean, modern, air-conditioned buses with a reliable timetable, an app that shows arrival times, and cheap monthly passes for students.
  • Roads: Build bypasses and more bridges, repair broken roads, and put smart traffic lights on the busiest junctions.
  • Limits on private vehicles: Charge for parking in the centre, or ban cars on certain streets at weekends.
  • Walking and cycling: Add wide pavements and protected bike lanes, and plant trees for shade.
  • Education and rules: Teach road safety in schools, and enforce fines for illegal parking and speeding.
  • Planning ahead: Move some schools and offices out of the centre, and let people work from home on some days.

Model answer (about 200 words)

If I could give my hometown some advice, the first thing I would say is that it needs to invest seriously in public transport. If the buses were modern, clean and always on time, and if there were an app showing exactly when the next one arrives, many people would happily leave their motorbikes at home. Offering cheap monthly tickets for students would also help a lot.

Secondly, the city should improve its roads. It could build a bypass so that traffic doesn't have to go through the centre, and install smart traffic lights that change according to how busy the junction is. In addition, charging higher parking fees in the centre would discourage people from driving there unless they really have to.

I also think the city should make it safer and more pleasant to walk and cycle. Wide pavements, shaded streets and protected bike lanes would encourage people to make short trips on foot. At the same time, the authorities should be stricter about illegal parking and teach children about road safety from a young age.

If the city does all of this, I'm confident that traffic would be much smoother, the air would be cleaner, and daily life would be a lot more relaxing for everyone.

3. Topic B: My hometown does not have many transport problems

Question 1: Why doesn't your hometown have many transport problems?

Idea bank

  • Size and layout: A small or medium town, with homes, schools, shops and the market all close together, so most trips are short.
  • Planning: Wide, well-maintained roads, ring roads that keep heavy trucks out of the centre, and enough parking.
  • Public transport: Frequent, cheap and punctual buses or trams, so people do not need their own car.
  • Habits: Many people walk, cycle or share rides, and drivers are patient and follow the rules.
  • Population and work: A stable population, with offices and factories spread across different areas, so there is no single rush-hour peak.
  • Effects: Short commutes, clean air, quiet streets and more time with family.

Model answer (about 190 words)

My hometown doesn't have many transport problems, and I think that's mainly because of its size and its design. It's a fairly small town, so most places that people need, such as schools, markets and offices, are within a few kilometres of each other. That means nobody has to travel very far, and the roads never get overloaded.

Another important factor is good planning. The main roads are wide and well maintained, and there is a ring road that keeps heavy trucks away from the centre. There is also plenty of parking, so drivers don't circle around looking for a space, which is a major cause of traffic jams in other places.

What's more, public transport works well. Buses run every ten minutes, they're cheap, and they almost always arrive on time. Many people also prefer to walk or ride a bike. For instance, I can get to my school in about fifteen minutes by bicycle, and I don't need to worry about traffic at all.

Finally, most drivers are patient and follow the rules, so accidents are rare. Because of all these things, travelling around my hometown is quick, safe and stress-free, and I really appreciate that.

Question 2: What advice do you have for other towns about their transport problems?

Idea bank

  • Plan early: Decide where homes, schools, offices and shops go before the town grows, and keep daily needs within a short walk or ride.
  • Public transport first: Make buses cheap, frequent and punctual, with dedicated bus lanes so they never get stuck in traffic.
  • Safe walking and cycling: Build separate bike lanes, safe crossings and car-free zones in the centre.
  • Smart technology: Use live traffic data, timed signals and a single app for tickets and timetables.
  • Culture and education: Teach road safety at school and reward good driving habits.
  • Learn from others: Copy what works in cities such as Amsterdam, Singapore or Copenhagen, and adapt it to local needs.

Model answer (about 200 words)

My main advice to other towns would be to think about transport early, before the problems appear. When planners decide where to build houses, schools and workplaces, they should keep them close together, so people don't need to make long journeys every day. In my hometown, this approach has kept the roads quiet even though the population is growing.

Secondly, I would recommend making public transport the easiest choice. If buses are cheap, frequent and have their own lanes, people will naturally use them instead of driving. A single app for timetables and tickets would make things even more convenient.

I would also suggest building safe cycling lanes and pedestrian areas. When people feel safe, they walk and cycle more, which reduces traffic, pollution and even healthcare costs. Teaching children about road safety at school is another simple step with long-term benefits.

Lastly, towns shouldn't be afraid to learn from successful places. Cities like Copenhagen have proved that cycling and good public transport can work together beautifully. If other towns follow these ideas and adapt them to their own needs, I'm sure they can avoid many of the traffic problems we see in big cities today.

5. Linking phrases and speaking tips

Linking phrases

  • Giving reasons: The main reason is that... / This is largely due to... / One important factor is...
  • Adding ideas: What's more, ... / On top of that, ... / Another thing is that...
  • Giving examples: To give you an example, ... / For instance, ... / A good example is...
  • Showing results: As a result, ... / Because of this, ... / That's why...
  • Giving advice: If I were in charge, I would... / It would be a good idea to... / The authorities should...
  • Closing: All in all, ... / To sum up, ... / That's why I believe...

Speaking tips

  • Aim for 5 to 7 sentences per question, about 1.5 to 2 minutes. Stop only when your idea feels complete.
  • Use real details such as street names, times and distances. They make your answer sound true and help you keep talking.
  • Mix sentence types: short statements, longer sentences with because or which, and one conditional (If the buses were better, people would...).
  • Do not memorise the model answers word for word. Memorise the structure and a few key phrases, then speak in your own words.
  • Slow down a little, pause between ideas, and stress key words such as traffic, public transport and rush hour.
  • Practice by recording yourself, then compare your answer with the idea bank to find ideas you missed.
